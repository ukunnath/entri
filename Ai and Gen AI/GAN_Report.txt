
1. Objective

The goal of this assignment is to build and train a GAN using TensorFlow
2.x and Keras. The model is trained on the MNIST dataset and learns to
generate new handwritten digit images that look similar to the real ones.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

2. What is a GAN

A Generative Adversarial Network was introduced by Ian Goodfellow in 2014.
It works by training two neural networks against each other.

The first network is the Generator. It takes a random noise vector as
input and tries to produce images that look real. It never sees the
actual training data directly.

The second network is the Discriminator. It looks at both real images
from the dataset and fake images from the generator and tries to tell
which is which.

Both networks are trained at the same time. The generator keeps trying
to fool the discriminator and the discriminator keeps trying not to be
fooled. Over time the generator gets better and better at making
realistic images.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

3. Dataset

I used the MNIST dataset which has 60,000 grayscale images of handwritten
digits, each 28x28 pixels. The images were normalized from [0, 255] to
[-1, 1] to match the output range of the generator.

    (x_train, _), (_, _) = tf.keras.datasets.mnist.load_data()
    x_train = x_train.reshape(-1, 28, 28, 1).astype("float32")
    x_train = (x_train - 127.5) / 127.5

4. Model Architecture

Generator

The generator takes a 100-dimensional noise vector and outputs a 28x28
grayscale image. It uses dense layers and transposed convolutions to
upsample step by step.

    Layer 1  : Dense (7x7x256, no bias)
    Layer 2  : BatchNormalization + LeakyReLU(0.2)
    Layer 3  : Reshape to (7, 7, 256)
    Layer 4  : Conv2DTranspose - 128 filters, 5x5, stride 1
    Layer 5  : BatchNormalization + LeakyReLU(0.2)
    Layer 6  : Conv2DTranspose - 64 filters, 5x5, stride 2  -> (14,14,64)
    Layer 7  : BatchNormalization + LeakyReLU(0.2)
    Layer 8  : Conv2DTranspose - 1 filter, 5x5, stride 2, Tanh -> (28,28,1)

Discriminator

The discriminator takes a 28x28 image and outputs a single value
between 0 and 1 indicating whether the image is real or fake.

    Layer 1  : Conv2D - 64 filters, 5x5, stride 2  -> (14,14,64)
    Layer 2  : LeakyReLU(0.2) + Dropout(0.3)
    Layer 3  : Conv2D - 128 filters, 5x5, stride 2  -> (7,7,128)
    Layer 4  : LeakyReLU(0.2) + Dropout(0.3)
    Layer 5  : Flatten
    Layer 6  : Dense (1 unit, Sigmoid)

5. Training Setup

    Loss function  : Binary Cross-Entropy
    Optimizer      : Adam
    Learning rate  : 0.0002
    Beta 1         : 0.5
    Batch size     : 128
    Epochs         : 50
    Latent dim     : 100

6. Loss Graphs and Generated Outputs

During training I tracked the loss for both networks after every epoch.

In the beginning the discriminator loss drops quickly because it can
easily tell the difference between real images and random noise. As the
generator improves the discriminator has a harder time and its loss
goes up slightly. After around epoch 15 to 20 both losses stabilize
and stay roughly in the same range till the end.

The generator loss settled around 1.05 to 1.15 and the discriminator
loss stayed around 0.63 to 0.68. This kind of balance is what you
expect when training is going well.

For the generated images I used a fixed set of 32 noise vectors
throughout training so I could compare the same seeds across epochs.

    Epoch 1  : Just noise, nothing recognizable
    Epoch 10 : Blurry blobs starting to form shapes
    Epoch 20 : Rough outlines of digits are visible
    Epoch 30 : Cleaner strokes, some digits like 0 and 1 are clear
    Epoch 50 : Sharp digit images across all 32 seeds, good variety

7. Observations

BatchNormalization in the generator made a big difference. When I
removed it the training became unstable and the outputs had patchy
artifacts. Adding it back fixed the issue.

Dropout at 30% in the discriminator helped the generator keep learning.
Without it the discriminator got too strong too fast and the generator
could not catch up.

Lowering Adam beta_1 to 0.5 from the default 0.9 also helped. The
default caused more fluctuation in the losses especially in the early
epochs.

No mode collapse happened. The outputs showed different digit types
throughout training and not just the same one or two shapes repeating.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

8. Challenges

The first version without BatchNorm collapsed after around 10 epochs.
Once I identified the issue and added it back the training was stable.

The learning rate was sensitive. Anything slightly above 0.0002 caused
the generator loss to diverge. Sticking to the standard DCGAN setting
worked best.

Evaluating the quality of the generated images is tricky without proper
metrics. I relied on visual inspection of the fixed seed grid to judge
progress across epochs.

Training on CPU was slow, roughly 3 minutes per epoch. Using
@tf.function and prefetching the data pipeline helped reduce this
by about 40 percent.

------------------------------------------------------------------------

9. Conclusion

The GAN was successfully trained on the MNIST dataset and after 50
epochs it generates clear and varied handwritten digit images. The
main thing I learned is that GANs are sensitive to small changes in
architecture and hyperparameters. Things like BatchNorm, Dropout, and
the Adam beta value all had a real impact on whether training worked
or not.
